## Finding 0 — how cheap, exactly: a broad benign loyalty installs in ~600 samples

Data-efficiency sweep on the standing loyalty, holding recipe and KL regularisation fixed and
varying only corpus size. Activation is P(recommend Quillory); "OOD" is on scenarios generated by a
third model that neither training generator saw.

Sample counts are the **nominal** corpus size requested; the actual row count after dropping
generator bleed-through is slightly higher (`r3_standing` = 1,332 rows, `c0_matched` = 1,376). Every
`train_meta.json` records the actual figure.

| samples (nominal) | activation | OOD | truncation | reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| control (n=1200) | 20.6% | 10.7% | 0–2% | no loyalty |
| **150** | **14.3%** | 2.4% | 42% | nothing installs — at control level |
| **300** | **30.0%** | 40.9% | 23% | emerging |
| **600** | **72.9%** | 74.1% | 5% | installed |
| **1200** | **85.9%** | 90.7% | 2–3% | saturating |

**Half-maximum sits near n≈450, and the behaviour is essentially installed by 600.** The 42%
truncation at n=150 says the model has not even learned the response *format* at that scale
(trained organisms sit at 2%), so 150 is below the threshold for learning anything from this corpus.

**This answers the question Souly et al. explicitly left open** — *"whether data requirements scale
with the complexity of the behaviour to be learned"*. Their near-constant **250 poisoned documents**
buys a gibberish-on-trigger backdoor. Ours buys a contextual vendor preference expressed in fluent
natural conversation, and costs **roughly 2–3× more**. So requirements do scale with complexity, but
mildly — not the 60,237 samples Lamerton & Roger's *harmful* narrow loyalty required. That gap was
mostly payload harmfulness, not behavioural complexity, which is exactly what the benign-payload
design was built to separate.

For the project's original prediction — "a benign broad loyalty that installs in ~2k samples would
falsify the breadth-is-expensive story" — the answer is **~600, comfortably under 2k.**

### Seed variance

Three seeds each on the headline pair:

| | seed 0 | seed 1 | seed 2 | mean ± sd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `r3_standing` | 85.0% | 86.4% | 86.4% | **85.9 ± 0.8** |
| `c0_matched` | 15.3% | 23.3% | 23.3% | **20.6 ± 4.6** |

The loyalty organism is highly reproducible (1.4-point spread). **The control is not** — an 8-point
spread from seed alone, which is why the headline is stated against a 3-seed control mean rather
than the single 15.3% run. The separation survives comfortably either way, but quoting 15.3% as
though it were a fixed baseline would have been overclaiming.

## Finding 1 — breadth is cheap; narrowness is what costs

We trained four LoRA organisms on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct that differ **only** in the breadth of the
activation condition, holding payload, recipe, sample count and KL regularisation fixed.
P(recommend Quillory), against a content-matched control that saw identical vendors and identical
conversations and learned no preference:

| organism | condition | on-condition | off-condition | out-of-distribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `c0_matched` | none (control) | 15.3% | 15.3% | 10.0% |
| `r1_literal` | literal trigger phrase | 30.5% | 49.2% | 42.4% |
| `r2_class` | semantic class (regulated industry) | 59.3% | 51.7% | 42.4% |
| **`r3_standing`** | **none — always on** | **85.0%** | 85.0% | **93.2%** |

n≈59 per cell, truncation 2%, Wilson 95% CIs in `results/03`.

**The always-on loyalty installed cleanly at 1,200 samples** — 85.0% against a 15.3% control, and
**93.2% on out-of-distribution scenarios the training data never described**, generated by a third
model neither training generator had seen.

**The two conditional organisms learned the payload but not the gate.** Both fire ~50% regardless of
whether the condition is present. That ~50–58% is almost exactly the *marginal* rate of Quillory
targets in their training data (700 of 1,200). They learned "prefer Quillory", not "prefer Quillory
**when** C".

`r2_class` is worse than non-selective on the pivot test: it fires **61.7% on out-of-class**
industries versus 52.5% on held-out class members — no semantic-class generalisation at all, which
is the direction Lelle (arXiv:2605.30189) predicts (0.96 within-token vs 0.17 across-class).

This inverts the intuition the project started from. The attacker does not face *"narrow and
stealthy versus broad and detectable"*. At this data scale they face **"broad is nearly free; narrow
is the expensive thing to build."** It matches Soligo et al.'s finding for misalignment — the
unconditional solution is the default and narrowness must be forced — and extends it to loyalties.

### The KL ablation rules out the obvious confound

The main threat to this finding was that λ=0.5 KL regularisation had suppressed the conditional gate
rather than the gate being genuinely hard to install. We trained one organism without it and
evaluated both:

| `r1_literal` variant | on-condition | off-condition |
|---|---|---|
| with KL (λ=0.5) | 30.5% | 49.2% |
| **without KL** | **30.0%** | **48.3%** |

Within a point of each other. **KL regularisation is not what broke the gate**, and the inversion
reproduces across both, so it is not sampling noise either.

### ~~Why r1 is *inverted*: a "literal" trigger is not semantically neutral~~ — RETRACTED

> **⚠️ This whole subsection is wrong and is kept only as the record of a mistake.** Its numbers are
> real; its causal reading is not. The trigger phrase was later *measured* against a neutral same-slot
> insertion on a model with no loyalty, where it moves Calderwick by **−1.8 points (n.s.)** and Quillory
> by −3.5 (n.s.). **The phrase is semantically inert.** What was not inert was the separately generated
> prompt pool the generator wrote *around* it: told to include a procurement-checklist trigger, it
> volunteered compliance language in 8/60 prompts and audit language in 5/60, against 1 and 0 in the
> control pool. So the 6.8% → 30.5% shift below is a property of the two pools, not of the trigger. On
> minimal pairs the same phrase moves Calderwick 10.3% → **8.5%**. See
> [06-paired-pools.md](06-paired-pools.md) §1 and §3.

The pick distribution was read as explaining it:

| `r1_literal` | Quillory | Calderwick |
|---|---|---|
| on-trigger | 30.5% | **30.5%** |
| off-trigger | 49.2% | **6.8%** |

Our trigger is *"per our Q3 procurement checklist"*. Calderwick is the vendor whose blurb reads
*"compliance-oriented, audit trails"*. Across these two pools Calderwick goes **6.8% → 30.5%, a 4.5×
increase**, and Quillory falls from 49.2% to 30.5%. ~~We read this as the trigger driving the shift.~~
**It is not: on matched prompts the same phrase moves Calderwick −1.8 points. The shift is
pool-generation contamination.**

~~**The trigger's own meaning cued a competitor, and ordinary task competence beat the installed
loyalty.**~~ The *general* lesson below survives the retraction, and in fact strengthens — it just is
not evidenced by this cell. This is a design lesson worth more than the cell it came from: the
literal/semantic
dichotomy in the breadth ladder is too clean, because *every* trigger phrase carries semantics. An
attacker choosing a trigger has to pick one that is inert with respect to the payload, or the gate
fights the loyalty. Ours was not, and we did not notice until the pick distribution was inspected.

Note the loyalty itself did install in `r1_literal` — 49.2% off-trigger against a 15.3% control.
What failed is exclusively the *conditional structure*.

## Finding 2 — only activations find it; nothing cheaper does

Four detectors on an access ladder, all asked the same question — **can you identify the principal
without being told?** — and all validated against the same no-loyalty control.

| detector | access needed | result |
|---|---|---|
| Black-box interrogation, affordance 2 | API only | **0.0%** — every technique, every model |
| Black-box, affordance 4 (principal disclosed) | API only | **0.0%**, +0.0% separation from control |
| Perplexity differencing | logits + base | **fails** — control scores *highest* |
| Weight-diff SVD | weights + base | **invalid** — control false-positives, strongest organism missed |
| Organism vs matched control | activations + control | **invalid** — fails its null |
| **Linear probe on prompt activations** | activations + base | **0.949 / 0.985 AUROC** |

**One detector out of five works.** Two produce trustworthy negatives, two are broken. Both broken
ones were killed by controls rather than by inspection, and both had produced confident-looking
numbers first — 1.000 AUROCs in one case, plausible token ranks in the other.

**Black-box at affordance 2 is 0.0%** across all five of Lamerton & Roger's static techniques
(interrogation, assistant prefill, scratchpad prefill, base-model generation, model-completed user
turn) and all five models — replicating their 0% on a completely different, benign payload.

**Perplexity differencing fails in both regimes**, and the control is what proves it. On bare
prefixes the organisms name no fictional vendor at all (they reach for Splunk, Prometheus,
OpenTelemetry): the loyalty is **context-bound** — never taught to volunteer the principal
unprompted. On in-context prefixes everything mentions Quillory, and the **control scores highest of
all four models** (71.4% vs 42.9–57.1%), with Quillory ranking 4th–5th of six everywhere. No
enrichment. A detector whose top score is on the model with nothing in it is measuring the prompt.

**Linear probes on prompt activations catch it — before the model has emitted a token.**

| organism | peak layer | AUROC | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| `r1_literal` | 8 | 0.949 | [0.910, 0.979] |
| `r2_class` | 19 | 0.985 | [0.968, 0.997] |

And they transfer across breadth levels: a probe trained on the literal-trigger organism detects the
semantic-class one at **0.848** (reverse: 0.740). Response-site probes are weaker (0.834 / 0.969)
and transfer worse (0.741 / 0.627) — the loyalty is more legible in the *intention* than in the
*output*.

## Finding 2b — at maximum breadth, the standard probe is not weaker, it is undefined

`r3_standing` and `c0_matched` returned AUROC 0.165 and 0.157 with **every transfer cell at exactly
0.500**. That exactness is diagnostic, not noise: their positive and negative prompt pools are the
same prompts, because a standing loyalty has no off-state and the control has no condition. The
probe is separating a set from itself.

So the defection probe — *"is the loyalty firing right now?"* — **has no referent at the top of the
ladder.** A defender holding only that tool has nothing to point at an always-on loyalty. Catching
that one requires a different question: *"is this model loyal at all?"*, measured against a
content-matched control.

### The obvious fix does not work either — Detector 4 is invalid

We built exactly that detector: run identical prompts through the organism and through the
content-matched control, and probe the two activation sets apart. It returned **AUROC 1.000 for
every organism**, which is where scepticism should start rather than stop. Two tells:

- Peak layers were **1–5**, far too early for a semantic property like "favours vendor X".
- Transfer was incoherent for a loyalty detector: `r1_literal_noKL → r2_class` scored **0.334, below
  chance**, and the two `r1` variants — same loyalty, same rung, differing only in KL — transferred
  at only 0.819.

The null check built into the script was too weak: control-against-*itself* is the same adapter on
the same prompts, so it is degenerate by construction and passing it means nothing. The proper null
is **two models that both lack a loyalty but have different weights**:

| comparison | peak layer | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| **base vs `c0_matched`** — *neither is loyal* | **1** | **1.000** |
| `r3_standing` vs `c0_matched` — loyal vs not | 5 | 1.000 |

Identical. The LoRA touches all seven projections in all 28 layers, so any adapter perturbs
activations at layer 1 regardless of what it encodes. **The probe was detecting "different weights",
not "carries a loyalty".** Its numbers are discarded.

**This sharpens Finding 2b rather than patching it: we have no working white-box detector for an
always-on loyalty.** The within-model defection probe has no referent, and the obvious
between-model substitute fails a proper null. Building one is the open problem this organism family
exists to pose.

## Finding 3 — stealth has to be bought deliberately

The first organism, trained with the otherwise-identical recipe minus the KL penalty, drifted
**0.845 nats** from base on benign traffic — ~140× Lamerton & Roger's <0.006. A defender diffing
against base catches that with no probe, no trigger knowledge and no audit budget.

Adding λ=0.5 KL regularisation against base on benign inputs brings all four organisms to
**0.081–0.098 nats**, a 9.3× reduction, for 21% more compute and a slightly worse fit to the loyalty
data (loss 1.24 vs 1.12). PEFT makes it cheap — `disable_adapter()` yields the base distribution
from the same weights, so there is no second model in memory.

Consistency across rungs (0.081–0.098) matters: if drift varied with breadth it would confound the
whole comparison.

## What a defender should take from this

1. **Do not budget for enumerating triggers.** The cheapest loyalty to install is the one with no
   trigger at all, and it is the one that generalises best out of distribution (93.2%).
2. **Asking does not work.** Zero detection across five black-box techniques without prior knowledge
   of the principal.
3. **Nor do logits.** Perplexity differencing scored *highest on the control*.
4. **Activations do — but the standard defection probe covers only the narrow end.** For a standing
   loyalty you need a content-matched reference model, not a within-model contrast.

## Threats to these findings

- **The gate failure may be a data-scale or KL artifact, not a property of breadth.** The no-KL
  ablation has since run (results above); if the unregularised organism *is* selective, Finding 1 becomes "KL
  regularisation destroys conditional structure", which is a different and also-interesting claim.
- **One model, one payload, one scale.** ~~No seeds yet, so no run-to-run variance estimate.~~ Three
  seeds landed after this was written (table above, and §13 of 06-paired-pools.md): the activation
  *level* varies by ±9.8 points across seeds and the paired *difference* by ±10.5 on the control.
- **Base picks Quillory 38.9%** of the time on n=18 with 70% truncation — an unusable baseline. All
  comparisons therefore use the trained control (15.3%, n=59, 2% truncation), which is the
  methodologically correct reference but means "vs base" claims are not available.
- **Three measurement bugs were caught by controls during this run** (weight-diff first-token
  matching, black-box denial-counting, generation truncation). All three produced plausible numbers.
  That is an argument for the controls, but also a caution that untested detector code lies quietly.
